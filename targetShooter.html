Target Shooter

Overview

Target Shooter is a fast-paced aim-training game I developed in one week. With such a short timeframe, I wanted to focus on what makes shooting feel good rather than trying to build a large or complex game.

I focused on responsive controls, clear hit feedback, and consistent pacing. Keeping the scope small helped me deliver a polished, functional experience within the time limit.

Features

Shooting Responsiveness

The shooting system is built around immediate feedback. When the player fires, a raycast registers the shot at once, making the controls feel fast and precise.

Targets react as soon as they are hit, reinforcing the connection between the player's input and the result. That direct response is central to making the shooting feel satisfying.

Feedback & Clarity

Clear feedback was central to the experience. A distinct sound confirms each successful shot, and targets disappear on impact. Their presentation also makes them easy to track.

These simple signals make each hit feel more rewarding. Moving targets add challenge and variety without changing the core shooting mechanic.

Reference-Based Design

I took inspiration from aim trainers such as KovaaK's FPS Aim Trainer, especially their emphasis on responsiveness and feedback. I applied those fundamentals through instant shots, clear audio confirmation, and simple, readable targets.

From there, I developed the presentation in my own direction while keeping the focus on the same clear and responsive shooting experience.

Development

Reverse Engineering the Core Mechanic

I began by recreating a simple shooting system to understand how it worked. I used a raycast to detect hits instantly, matching the responsiveness I wanted from an aim trainer.

I then added basic feedback, including hit sounds and target reactions. Even at this early stage, the gameplay felt close to my goal, showing me how much a strong core mechanic can achieve.

Rapid Prototyping & Iteration

Because the project had a short timeframe, I tested ideas quickly rather than spending too long planning. I prototyped target spawning, movement, and shooting interactions, then adjusted or removed anything that didn't feel right.

I also tuned details such as fire rate, spawn timing, and target speed. Testing these values helped me create a more balanced and satisfying experience.

Focus on Core Over Complexity

I kept the visuals minimal early on so I could focus on how the shooting felt. Once the mechanics were working well, I refined the presentation.

This order kept development focused on what mattered most: making a simple mechanic feel as good as possible before adding polish.

Key Learnings

Responsiveness is critical. Even a few frames of input delay can make shooting feel sluggish. Immediate responses make the experience feel precise and satisfying.

Feedback drives satisfaction. Sound and visual reactions reinforce successful actions. Every hit needs immediate confirmation.

Simple mechanics can be powerful. I worried the core mechanic might be too basic, but testing showed me that one well-executed idea can be more effective than many underdeveloped features.

Rapid iteration saves time. Testing ideas quickly and discarding what doesn't work keeps development moving. It's more valuable to test several versions in a few hours than to spend days perfecting an idea that doesn't feel right.

Build the core gameplay before polishing the visuals. Keeping visuals minimal let me focus on the shooting. Once the mechanic felt solid, I could add polish to support an already enjoyable experience.
