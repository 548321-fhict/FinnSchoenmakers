Sublevel

Overview

Sublevel is a horror exploration game set in an abandoned underground bunker. I designed and built the full level layout, with a focus on spatial design, atmospheric lighting, and environmental storytelling.

My goal was to create an immersive, unsettling experience where players uncover the space without explicit guidance. I used pacing, lighting, and subtle environmental clues to guide them through the bunker and shape how each area feels.

Features

Spatial Design & Player Flow

I designed corridors and rooms to guide players through visual cues rather than explicit markers. Branching paths and loops make the bunker feel larger and less predictable, building suspense as players decide where to go next.

The layout shifts between tight corridors and narrow corners and calmer, open spaces. This contrast controls pacing and emotional intensity, while making players question where they are and reinforcing the horror atmosphere.

Atmospheric Lighting System

I placed flickering lights near key areas to guide players while keeping them uneasy. Cold, desaturated corridor lighting creates a sterile, eerie mood, while warmer pools of light offer a sense of psychological safety.

Dynamic shadows add depth and unpredictability, and post-processing effects such as film grain and bloom create a gritty, cinematic look. I tested the lighting in gameplay, where it looked and felt very different from the editor, then adjusted it to suit the player's view.

Environmental Storytelling

I used props to hint at the bunker's history: overturned chairs, scattered papers, broken equipment, and blood trails. These details invite players to imagine what happened before they arrived.

Areas such as the flooded room and laboratory add mystery and dread without relying on exposition. Fog and floating dust give the spaces more depth, making flashlight beams more prominent and helping the bunker feel immersive.

Structural Detail & Asset Placement

Pipes, vents, cables, and broken panels help the bunker feel functional yet decayed. I carefully calibrated the scale and spacing of assets to create a sense of claustrophobia without obstructing navigation.

These smaller details work alongside the lighting, fog, and dust to make the bunker feel lived-in, but unsafe.

Development

Process

I began with layout sketches to map the level's structure and player flow. I then built graybox blockouts in Unreal using simple geometry, which let me test navigation and scale before adding detail.

Repeated playtests revealed that the lighting looked very different in gameplay than it did in the editor. I used what I learned from those tests to rework shadow placement and brightness levels.

Key Learnings

Pacing through space can matter more than mechanics. Alternating tight and open areas, adding loops and branching paths, and using subtle lighting changes can create tension without adding more game systems.

Graybox blockouts reveal problems early. Testing navigation and scale with simple geometry before adding detail saves time and helps prevent major redesigns.

Playtesting from the player's perspective is essential. A level can feel very different in gameplay than it does in the editor. I had to rework the lighting twice after testing it in-game.

Environmental storytelling is powerful. Props and spatial design can tell a story without dialogue. When players fill in the gaps themselves, the horror can feel more personal.

Lighting is a level design tool, not just decoration. Light direction, color, and flicker patterns can guide players and shape how they feel.
